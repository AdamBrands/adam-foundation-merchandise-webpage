From a global conversation
to everyday action.

Adam Foundation is launching Kawthar during COP31, connecting the global conversation about our planet with practical action in UK mosques this Ramadan.

Alongside reducing reliance on disposable water bottles, the campaign opens a welcoming conversation about Islam: what it teaches about the natural world, how we treat one another, and the responsibilities that come with what we have.

The launch begins the invitation. Ramadan puts it into practice. The learning and care continue all year.

Discover the values behind Kawthar

Rooted in Islam.
Relevant to everyday life.

You do not need to be Muslim, or know anything about Islam, to explore these ideas. Start with something we all recognise: a drink of water, a shared meal, a living tree.

For Muslims, faith shapes how we use resources and how we treat people and living things. For everyone, these teachings offer an opportunity to learn, reflect and find common ground.

For Muslims, caring for Allah’s creation is a shared duty: to avoid waste, prevent harm and act responsibly, according to our ability and circumstances.

Allah is the Arabic word for God. Care for creation gives everyday choices moral significance—from the water we pour to the things we buy and throw away. Kawthar is one practical way to express that responsibility.

أمانة

Amanah · Trust

What we have is entrusted to us.

In Islamic ethics, a trust brings responsibility. Applying that principle to the environment means caring for resources rather than treating them as endlessly disposable.

Explore the idea

The Qur’an describes people as successors on earth, tested through what they are given. This offers a basis for reflecting on stewardship and accountability.

Try it: Care for something you already own so it lasts longer.

Read Qur’an 6:165 ↗
بلا إسراف

Avoiding israf · Waste and excess

Enjoy the blessing. Avoid the waste.

Islam welcomes the enjoyment of food and drink while teaching moderation. Appreciating a resource includes being thoughtful about how much we use.

Explore the idea

“Eat and drink, but do not waste.” This Qur’anic instruction connects enjoying provision with restraint.

Try it: Pour the water you need, finish it, and refill when you want more.

Read Qur’an 7:31 ↗
شكر

Shukr · Gratitude

Gratitude can become a habit.

A glass of clean water is easy to take for granted. Gratitude invites us to notice what sustains us and respond with thoughtful actions.

Explore the idea

The Qur’an calls people to gratitude. The campaign applies that teaching by encouraging care for the resources we receive.

Try it: Pause before drinking to appreciate the water and the people who made it available.

Read Qur’an 14:7 ↗
رحمة

Rahmah · Mercy

Kindness reaches beyond ourselves.

Islamic teachings recognise the value of kindness to animals. Compassion asks us to notice the needs of living things that cannot speak for themselves.

Explore the idea

A well-known account from the Prophet Muhammad’s teachings describes a man being forgiven after giving water to a thirsty dog.

Try it: Consider how litter and discarded packaging affect wildlife where you live.

Read Sahih al-Bukhari 2363 ↗
صدقة

Sadaqah · Charity

Goodness can keep growing.

Giving is about more than money. Planting something that nourishes people, birds or animals can itself be an act of charity.

Explore the idea

The Prophet Muhammad taught that planting a tree or sowing a crop from which others eat counts as charity for the Muslim who plants it.

Try it: Help care for a community garden or support planting that suits your local environment.

Read Sahih al-Bukhari 2320 ↗

We can do good together.

Care becomes more effective when people cooperate. Mosques, neighbours, businesses and volunteers can each contribute something useful.

Explore the idea

The Qur’an encourages cooperation in goodness. Kawthar puts this into practice through a shared invitation to fund bottles, enable refills and support lasting habits.

Why begin with Ramadan?

Ramadan is the month in which Muslims fast and devote particular attention to worship, generosity and reflection. During Taraweeh—the additional night prayers—mosques often provide drinking water for worshippers.

Kawthar builds on that generosity. A bottle people can refill and keep offers a practical alternative to repeatedly taking disposable bottles, during Ramadan and afterwards.

A name with a deeper meaning.

Kawthar means abundant goodness. In Islamic tradition, it is also the name of a river in Paradise granted to the Prophet Muhammad.

The name brings a gentle reminder of blessing and gratitude. Our environmental message draws on Islam’s wider teachings about responsibility, moderation and care.

Put the values into practice

01

Bring it.

Take your bottle to the mosque, work, school or wherever your day leads. Let it become part of your routine.

02

Refill it.

Use safe drinking-water refill points. Each time your refill replaces a disposable bottle, you avoid that item of single-use packaging.

03

Keep the goodness going.

Clean and care for your bottle. Scan its QR code to learn, explore the Foundation and share the campaign with someone new.

The benefit comes from repeated use. Keep using a suitable bottle you already own; receiving a new one is not necessary to take part.

A little more context

Your questions,
answered.

Do I need to be Muslim to take part?

No. The campaign is inspired by Islam and welcomes people of every faith and none to learn, contribute and support environmental care. The initial bottle distribution is through UK mosques during Ramadan.

Is caring for the environment a religious responsibility?

For Muslims, duties such as avoiding waste, preventing harm and respecting Allah’s creation give environmental care a moral and religious foundation. How that responsibility is fulfilled depends on ability and circumstances. Joining this particular campaign is one possible action, not itself a religious obligation.

What is happening during COP31?

Adam Foundation is launching Kawthar during COP31 in Türkiye, inviting partners and mosques to help turn environmental concern into practical action for Ramadan and beyond.

What will I find when I scan the QR code?

The bottle and accompanying leaflet will link to this campaign page: accessible learning, ways to participate, the Foundation’s wider work and a request form for the free digital adhkar book. Reading the educational content does not require you to give us your details.

Can a mosque apply without refill facilities already in place?

Yes. Tell us what is available and where you need help. A practical plan for access to safe drinking water will be important to making the bottles useful.

How will the campaign understand its impact?

The aim is to track bottles distributed, participating mosques and feedback on continued use and refill arrangements. Distribution alone does not prove an environmental saving; repeated replacement of disposable bottles is what matters.
